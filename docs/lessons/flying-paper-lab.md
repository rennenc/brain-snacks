# מעבדת הנייר המעופף · The Flying Paper Lab

Why does a 400-ton plane stay in the air? A Hebrew, hands-on lesson for ages 8–12, about an
hour long, that needs only 4–5 sheets of A4 paper.

Play: https://brainsnacks.pages.dev/lessons/flying-paper-lab/ · Code: [`site/lessons/flying-paper-lab/index.html`](../../site/lessons/flying-paper-lab/index.html)

**Design:** a lab notebook on engineering graph paper. One column of mission cards; the two
simulators run full width like instruments on a bench.

## Missions

Every experiment starts with a **prediction** (multiple choice), then the child tries it and
reads why.

1. **Air is not nothing**: a crumpled ball vs. a flat sheet falling (drag).
2. **The bridge that won't fly**: blowing under a paper bridge flattens it (fast air presses less).
3. **The floating strip**: blowing over a paper strip lifts it (air following a curved surface).
4. **Two sheets that pull together**: blowing between two sheets.
5. **Bernoulli tube** simulator: squeeze a pipe and watch speed and pressure gauges change.
6. **Inside a wing** wind tunnel: angle of attack and speed vs. lift and drag, stall, the
   "equal transit time" myth busted with a particle race, and the four forces.
7. **The test plane**: fold a paper plane, change one thing (the elevons), measure three throws
   each, and let the flight log chart pick the winner.
8. **The falling helicopter** (bonus): a paper spinner, made by folding and tearing only.

It ends with a 4-question pilot quiz and a certificate with the child's name.

## Notes

- The physics is careful: lift comes from turning air downward (Newton) *together with* lower
  pressure above the wing (Bernoulli). These are two descriptions of one thing, not rival ones.
- The simulators keep the right relationships (lift grows with speed², rises with angle until
  a stall around 15°) without pretending to be exact.
- Progress, checklists, the flight log and the quiz are saved in the browser.
